Mirror-image tests reveal hemispheric asymmetries that alter whole-brain connectome simulations

Kazushi Takehana1
1Affiliation to be completed by the author. Correspondence: Kazushi Takehana.

Whole-brain connectomes allow spiking models of an entire insect brain to be built from wiring alone1–3, and their predictions can be confirmed experimentally3. Yet connectomes contain reconstruction errors — missed and spurious synapses, truncated neurites and mispredicted neurotransmitters1,4,5 — with unknown effects on simulated activity. Here we use the two hemispheres of FlyWire as built-in replicates. Simulated from the right instead of the left hemisphere, all six published benchmark predictions of a whole-brain Drosophila model3 failed, and sugar-receptor stimulation drove about 8,600 of 138,639 neurons into runaway excitation. Four left–right asymmetries, probably reconstruction errors, account for most failures: weaker sugar-receptor outputs, a grooming interneuron with a quarter of its homologue's inputs, a single sugar-receptor neuron wired into the antennal lobe, and 40 antennal-lobe neurons predicted with low confidence to be serotonergic, which act as excitatory amplifiers. Correcting three of them restores four predictions. Error injection shows that predictions tolerate randomly placed spurious synapses but that most halve when 14–24% of synapses are missing. Across 131 sensory cell types, left and right responses agree far less than repeated runs (median r = 0.58 versus 0.99). Mirror tests offer a simple control for connectome-based simulations.

Main

Dense electron-microscopy reconstructions now cover the central brain6 and the entire brain of the adult fruit fly1,2 and, more recently, its brain and ventral nerve cord together7,8. These wiring diagrams have been turned directly into simulations. A leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model of the FlyWire brain with a single free parameter predicted which neurons respond to taste and mechanosensory input, and 91% of 164 tested predictions were confirmed experimentally3. Connectome-constrained models of the visual system make similar progress9,10. The appeal is that a wiring diagram, plus generic neuronal properties, may be sufficient to predict function, although how far anatomy constrains activity remains debated11–13.

Wiring diagrams are, however, measurements with errors. Automated synapse detection in the full adult fly brain (FAFB) volume reaches an F-score of about 0.74 (ref. 5); only 44.7% of postsynaptic sites in FlyWire are attached to proofread neurons1; most tracing errors are omitted fine branches14; neurotransmitter identity is predicted from images with 87–94% accuracy, falling to 33% for serotonin4; and roughly 40% of connections found in one hemisphere are absent from the other2. Shiu et al. tested how their predictions depend on global parameters3, but how specific reconstruction errors propagate into simulated activity has not been measured, and most published simulations stimulated neurons in one hemisphere3.

The bilateral symmetry of the fly brain offers a natural control. Homologous left and right neurons share cell types and, for strong connections, more than 90% of their partners2, and the two hemispheres of a larval connectome have been compared as paired network samples15. A prediction that reflects the biology of a circuit should therefore hold when the same experiment is simulated from the mirror-image side of the same brain. Here we performed this mirror test for the benchmark predictions of the FlyWire whole-brain model, identified left–right asymmetries responsible for the failures we found, and quantified how seven types of reconstruction error alter model predictions.

Results

A mirror test for a whole-brain spiking model

We re-implemented the LIF model of Shiu et al.3 as an event-driven simulator that runs many trials in parallel on a graphics processing unit (GPU; Methods). With the published equations and constants it reproduced the original Brian 2 implementation16,17 neuron by neuron (Pearson r ≥ 0.999 for 409–8,856 active neurons in three conditions; at 150 Hz the two MN9 motor neurons fired at 56.7 and 85.8 Hz versus 56.6 and 85.9 Hz; Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). A 1-s trial of the 138,639-neuron FlyWire v783 brain, with 15.1 million connections and 54.5 million synapses, took about 0.2 s. For the 362 neurons that kept their identifiers between proofreading versions v630 and v783 and fired in either, responses to the original sugar neurons of Shiu et al. correlated at r = 0.997, although MN9 fired 12% less in v783 (Extended Data Fig. 1d).

We defined each stimulus and readout by cell type and hemisphere so that every benchmark could be run from either side (Methods). The benchmarks were six predictions of Shiu et al. that were confirmed experimentally3,18,19: sugar gustatory receptor neurons (GRNs) activate the proboscis motor neuron MN9; bitter GRNs and Ir94e GRNs suppress this activation; and Johnston's organ (JO) neurons activate the antennal-grooming brain neuron aBN1 (ipsilaterally) and the antennal-grooming descending neurons aDN1 and aDN2 (contralaterally). Our stimuli comprise complete cell types — for example, all 33 left sugar GRNs of types LB3b and LB3c20 and all 229 left JO-C and JO-E neurons — and therefore differ from the neuron sets selected by Shiu et al. (21 sugar and 70 JO-CE neurons); we examine those sets below.

Right-hemisphere predictions fail

Stimulated from the left hemisphere, the model reproduced all six benchmarks (Fig. 1). Left sugar GRNs drove MN9 from a stimulation rate of 50 Hz, reaching 58 Hz at 100 Hz; adding bitter or Ir94e GRNs suppressed this response by 89% and 78%; and JO neurons drove aBN1 at 68 Hz and the contralateral aDN1 and aDN2 at 29 and 33 Hz (Fig. 1b,e,f).

The same simulations run from the right hemisphere failed in every case (Fig. 1e,f). Right sugar GRNs (24 neurons) evoked less than 1 Hz in MN9 at 100 Hz and only 17 Hz at 300 Hz (Fig. 1b). Instead, from 100 Hz onwards the network entered a runaway state in which 8,612 neurons fired in at least one of eight trials, 21 times the 412 neurons recruited from the left (Fig. 1a,c). Runaway began abruptly, at 59, 135 and 414 ms in three trials, after a period during which the right response resembled the left (Fig. 1d). The original Brian 2 code reproduced the runaway (8,742 active non-stimulated neurons; MN9 at 0.55 Hz), ruling out an implementation error. Right JO neurons drove the ipsilateral aBN1 at only 11 Hz, and the contralateral descending neurons not at all at any rate up to 300 Hz (Extended Data Fig. 4a). The failure depends partly on which right-hemisphere neurons are stimulated: the nine right sugar neurons that Shiu et al. selected (types LB3c and LB4b) drove MN9 without runaway at 150 Hz and above, although less strongly than their left set (29 versus 66 Hz at 150 Hz; Extended Data Fig. 4b).

Under-connected sensory neurons and an under-connected readout

Right gustatory neurons differ systematically from their left homologues in the connectome. Each right sugar GRN has a mean of 125 output synapses, compared with 265 on the left (ratio 0.47); bitter, Ir94e and water GRNs show ratios of 0.64, 0.73 and 0.36, whereas JO neurons show no deficit (1.18; Fig. 2a). Together with nine fewer annotated neurons, the right sugar GRNs provide 3,000 output synapses against 8,800 on the left.

To test whether this deficit can account for the loss of MN9 activation, we removed randomly chosen output synapses of the left sugar GRNs. MN9 activation declined steeply once the total fell below about 5,000 synapses, and at the right-hemisphere total of 3,000 synapses the left curve predicted 1–10 Hz, matching the 1.3 Hz observed from the right (Fig. 2b; both with the antennal-lobe correction described below). For this readout, the right sugar population therefore behaves like a left population with missing synapses. Because strong homologous connections in FlyWire agree closely between hemispheres2 and improved synapse detection increases bilateral symmetry21, a twofold deficit across a sensory population is more likely a reconstruction error, such as incompletely proofread axons or missed synapses, than a biological asymmetry, but our data cannot exclude the latter.

The grooming failure has a different source. The right aBN1, the readout of the JO benchmark, has 459 input synapses, a quarter of the 1,833 on its left homologue, and receives 35 rather than 103 synapses directly from JO neurons (Fig. 2c). Here the asymmetry lies on the readout side of the circuit.

A single wiring anomaly and mispredicted transmitters create runaway

The synapse deficit does not explain the runaway: in the published connectome, removing up to 90% of the left GRN output synapses never produced it (at most 418 active neurons). Two further asymmetries do. First, leave-one-out stimulation of the 24 right sugar GRNs identified a single neuron (LB3b, FlyWire 720575940629884119) whose omission abolished the runaway (58 instead of 8,522 active neurons; Extended Data Fig. 4c). This neuron makes 22 of its 154 output synapses onto antennal-lobe local neurons of type il3LN6, whereas left LB3b neurons make at most three such synapses (Extended Data Fig. 4d). The nine right sugar neurons selected by Shiu et al. do not include it, which explains why they did not run away.

Second, the activity entering the antennal lobe was amplified by local neurons of types lLN2T_b, lLN2T_c and lLN2T_e, after which it spread across the central brain. These neurons are predicted to release serotonin, which the model treats as excitatory3, and their predictions are uncertain: the 40 antennal-lobe local neurons labelled serotonergic, 20 in each hemisphere, have a median confidence of 0.35, lower than that of local neurons labelled GABAergic, glutamatergic or cholinergic (Fig. 2d). Antennal-lobe local neurons are predominantly GABAergic, with a minority of glutamatergic and cholinergic cells22, and the only serotonergic neuron known to innervate the antennal lobe is the contralaterally projecting CSD neuron23,24, which is not a local neuron. Making only these 40 neurons inhibitory abolished the runaway (236 instead of 8,680 active neurons) without changing any left-hemisphere benchmark (Fig. 2e,f). Because this amplifier exists in both hemispheres, it is the right-specific entry route that explains why runaway occurred only from the right.

A blunter correction illustrates why sign assumptions must be treated as hypotheses. Making all 2,201 predicted serotonergic neurons inhibitory also prevented the runaway but abolished the left-hemisphere bitter suppression (MN9 at 59 Hz without and 58 Hz with bitter input), because 7 of the 21 left bitter GRNs are themselves predicted to be serotonergic. On the right, 19 of the 21 bitter GRNs carry this label, so the same cell types receive different transmitter predictions in the two hemispheres.

Correcting three asymmetries restores four predictions

With the antennal-lobe sign correction alone, the right hemisphere still reproduced none of the six benchmarks, because the runaway was replaced by a weak response. Scaling the output weights of each right sensory population to the left per-neuron mean (×2.12 for sugar, ×1.57 for bitter and ×1.38 for Ir94e GRNs) restored MN9 activation (47 Hz) and its suppression by bitter (85%) and Ir94e (93%) input. Scaling the inputs of right readout neurons to their left totals (×3.99 for aBN1 and ×1.06 for MN9) restored activation of aBN1 by JO neurons (74 Hz versus 68 Hz on the left; Fig. 2f). Contralateral activation of aDN1 and aDN2 remained absent (3 Hz), and right sugar stimulation still recruited more neurons than left stimulation (1,044 versus 429), indicating further differences that we did not identify. These corrections test sufficiency only, using the left hemisphere as the reference.

Missing synapses and sign errors, not spurious synapses, degrade predictions

To measure how each kind of reconstruction error propagates into predictions, we injected seven types of error into the connectome and re-ran the left-hemisphere benchmarks (three random draws per level; Fig. 3). Predictions were insensitive to randomly placed spurious synapses: adding 80% more synapses between partners drawn in proportion to their existing connectivity left every benchmark unchanged (Fig. 3d). They were sensitive to missing synapses. Removing synapses at random halved the activation of aDN1 at 14%, of MN9 at 21% and of aBN1 at 45%; at 30% the MN9 response fell to 8% of intact, and at 40% it was abolished (Fig. 3a,h). Truncating dendrites or axons — removing a random fraction of each neuron's inputs or outputs — had similar effects (Fig. 3b,c). Bitter and Ir94e suppression remained intact for as long as sugar still drove MN9, beyond which they could not be evaluated.

Random sign errors were tolerated at low rates. When 1–2% of neurons switched between excitatory and inhibitory, mean benchmark responses stayed within 15% of intact, although single draws deviated by up to 50%; at 8–18%, four of the five benchmarks lost half of their effect, and at 20% two of three draws ran away (Fig. 3e). For errors drawn from the transmitter classifier's own confidence, a scale that changed the transmitter of 8.2% and the sign of 6.1% of neurons — comparable to the classifier's reported per-neuron error of about 6% (ref. 4) — left all benchmarks within 20% of intact in two of three draws, whereas the third ran away and lost most aDN1 activation (Fig. 3f). Such calibrated errors were not more damaging on average than random sign errors of similar size, but they triggered runaway more often (one of three draws versus none at 5% random flips). Variability of synaptic strength around the synapse count, with the mean weight held constant, was largely tolerated at a log-normal σ of 0.25 (aDN1 activation −25%); at 0.5, aDN1 activation fell by 56% and one of three draws ran away, and at σ = 1 every draw ran away (Fig. 3g). Analysis conventions mattered as well: discarding connections with fewer than five synapses, a common threshold1, reduced JO-evoked activation of aDN1 by 83% and of aBN1 by 46%, and treating glutamate as excitatory drove 30,128 neurons into runaway and reversed both suppression benchmarks, as anticipated by Shiu et al.3 (Extended Data Fig. 2b,c). Brain-wide similarity to the intact response declined gradually even where benchmarks failed abruptly (Extended Data Fig. 2a).

These dose–response curves help explain why the asymmetries of Fig. 2 matter. The model's single weight parameter was tuned to the connectome as reconstructed3, so a deficit shared uniformly by the whole brain would be partly absorbed by that calibration, whereas a deficit confined to one circuit is not. Removing synapses only from the left sugar-receptor neurons halved the MN9 response at a loss of about 50% (Fig. 2b), and the right sugar-receptor neurons provide 66% fewer output synapses in total than the left.

A brain-wide mirror screen

To ask whether hemispheric disagreement is specific to the benchmark circuits, we stimulated each of 131 sensory cell types — every type with at least three annotated neurons on each side — from the left and from the right (Methods). In the published connectome, 60 types (46%) drove more than 2,000 non-stimulated neurons from at least one side, including 52 of the 53 olfactory receptor neuron types (Fig. 4a). After the 40-neuron antennal-lobe sign correction, this occurred for 8 olfactory types (6%), and after the additional cell-type consensus correction, for one type, photoreceptors R1–6, whose 4,029–4,423 stimulated neurons per side drive much of the optic lobes. The antennal-lobe amplifier therefore accounts for most of the runaway behaviour of the published model.

Even without runaway, mirror-image stimulation produced markedly different brain-wide responses. We compared, cell type by cell type, the responses evoked from the left and from the right with those obtained when the same side was stimulated twice with new random input. Excluding types that ran away, repeated stimulation gave nearly identical responses (median r = 0.99), whereas left and right stimulation agreed far less (median r = 0.58, interquartile range 0.43–0.72; P = 4 × 10−21, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 118 types; Fig. 4b). For 85 of these 118 types, mirror agreement was lower than the lowest agreement between repeats. Discordance was not predicted by the asymmetry of the stimulated population's own synapse totals (Spearman ρ = −0.01, P = 0.9; Fig. 4c) or of its predicted transmitter signs (ρ = −0.06, P = 0.5), and making transmitter signs consistent within every cell type improved it only slightly (median r = 0.62 versus 0.58, P = 0.01, n = 115). One systematic component was lateral: for olfactory types, the share of evoked activity in the stimulated hemisphere was lower for right than for left stimulation (median 0.45 versus 0.53, P = 0.002, n = 45), whereas mechanosensory types showed no such bias (0.89 versus 0.87, P = 0.44, n = 39; Fig. 4d).

Discussion

Simulating the same experiment from the two hemispheres of one connectome is a control that requires no new data and no assumption beyond bilateral symmetry. Applied to a whole-brain model whose predictions had been validated experimentally3, it showed that all six benchmarks, as defined here by complete cell types, would not have been reproduced from the right hemisphere. The failures were not subtle: the right hemisphere failed to drive a motor neuron that the left drove at nearly 60 Hz and instead recruited 6% of the brain in runaway excitation.

The asymmetries we identified suggest practical checks. Whole sensory populations can be under-connected, as were the right gustatory receptor neurons; comparing per-neuron synapse totals of stimulated populations between hemispheres is trivial and would have flagged this. Readout neurons can be under-connected, as was the right aBN1. A single neuron with unusual connections can open a path into another circuit, and neurotransmitter predictions of low confidence can turn that circuit into an amplifier; in a model without adaptation or other stabilizing mechanisms, the combination produced runaway. Network-wide activity should therefore be monitored in every simulation, not only the readout neurons, and conclusions that depend on low-confidence transmitter predictions — especially serotonin, the transmitter predicted least accurately4 — should be tested by varying those signs.

The dose–response analysis indicates where reconstruction effort matters most for simulation. Randomly placed spurious synapses were harmless even at high rates, presumably because they are scattered and weak, whereas missing synapses degraded predictions steeply, so completeness matters more than precision. Missing synapses are also the dominant error in automated reconstructions1,5. Sign errors at the rate implied by the classifier's accuracy left most predictions intact but could trigger runaway, and their effect depended on which neurons were affected: 40 low-confidence predictions in one circuit mattered more than thousands of random flips elsewhere. The brain-wide screen shows that the benchmark failures were not isolated. Responses to most sensory types differed between hemispheres far more than between repeated runs, and the discordance tracked neither the synapse totals nor the predicted signs of the stimulated neurons, which suggests that it arises from asymmetries distributed through downstream circuits. A whole-brain prediction in this model is thus partly a property of the hemisphere in which it is computed. Comparable cross-connectome replications are beginning to appear for the ventral nerve cord25 and for other uses of this model26, and mirror tests could accompany them at no cost.

Our analysis has limitations. We cannot know which hemisphere is closer to the biological truth; the left side may contain errors that happen to favour the published predictions, and our corrections rescale weights coarsely rather than restore missing synapses. Whether the right LB3b neuron's connections to the antennal lobe reflect a merge or synapse-assignment error or genuine anatomy is unknown, and the antennal-lobe sign correction, although supported by anatomy22–24, should be confirmed by immunostaining or transcriptomics of the lLN2T types. Benchmark outcomes depend on how stimuli are defined and on stimulation rate (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). The model omits gap junctions, neuromodulation, adaptation and cell-type-specific membrane properties3,11; these simplifications apply equally to both hemispheres but may amplify differences between them. Newer synapse predictions that improve bilateral symmetry21 and the brain-and-nerve-cord connectomes7,8 offer independent replicates against which the mirror test can be repeated.

Connectome-based simulations are becoming a standard way to generate hypotheses about neural circuits. Our results suggest a simple practice for them: run every prediction from both hemispheres, report the agreement, monitor brain-wide activity, and treat sign assumptions for low-confidence neurons as parameters to be varied. Disagreement between the two halves of the same brain may indicate where the wiring diagram, rather than the biology, is shaping a prediction.

Methods

Connectome data

We used the FlyWire v783 connectome1,2 as distributed with the model of Shiu et al.3,17: 138,639 neurons and 15,091,983 connections carrying 54,492,922 synapses, each connection with its synapse count and the predicted sign of its presynaptic neuron. A neuron is inhibitory if its predicted transmitter4 is GABA or glutamate and excitatory otherwise (acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin and octopamine). Cell types, hemisphere, predicted transmitter, its confidence and positions were taken from the FlyWire annotations2 (Supplementary file 1, flyconnectome/flywire_annotations, accessed 27 October 2026). Hemisphere labels follow these annotations; the sugar neurons that Shiu et al. called right-hemisphere are labelled left in this release. For the version comparison we used FlyWire v630 (127,400 neurons) from the same repository.

Whole-brain model

The model follows Shiu et al.3. Each neuron obeys dv/dt = (v0 − v + g)/τm and dg/dt = −g/τs, with resting and reset potential v0 = −52 mV, threshold −45 mV, τm = 20 ms, τs = 5 ms and a refractory period of 2.2 ms during which v and g are not integrated. At a spike, v is reset and g set to zero. A presynaptic spike increments g by n × wsyn after 1.8 ms, where n is the signed synapse count and wsyn = 0.275 mV. Stimulated neurons receive Poisson input that adds 250 × wsyn to v and have no refractory period. Neurons have no spontaneous activity. Trials last 1 s with a time step of 0.1 ms.

Our simulator (Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.14 with CUDA 13.0, one NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti) integrates the linear subthreshold dynamics exactly, applies the update order of Brian 2 (integration, threshold, synaptic events, reset) and runs a batch of independent trials in parallel; 30 trials of 1 s took 6 s. Synaptic input is delivered event by event from a compressed column representation of the weight matrix, so the cost scales with the number of spikes. Comparing voltage traces with Brian 2 showed that synaptic input arriving during the refractory period does not persist in the original implementation; we reproduced this behaviour. For validation we ran the original code17 (Brian 2.10 with Cython code generation16) with identical stimuli for 10–30 trials and compared firing rates neuron by neuron, excluding stimulated neurons.

Stimuli, readouts and benchmarks

Populations were defined by FlyWire cell type and hemisphere: sugar GRNs, LB3b and LB3c (33 left, 24 right neurons); bitter GRNs, LB1a–d (21, 21); Ir94e-like GRNs, LB1e and LB2a–c (18, 20), following the type composition of the Ir94e set of Shiu et al.; water GRNs, LB3a (15, 15); and JO neurons, all JO-C and JO-E types (229, 204). Readouts were MN9 (type CB0701), aBN1 (SAD093), aDN1 (DNg62) and aDN2 (DNge078), one neuron per hemisphere each. The original neuron sets of Shiu et al. were mapped to v783 by root identifier (20 of 21 left and 9 of 10 right sugar neurons present). Gustatory benchmarks used 100-Hz stimulation and JO benchmarks 150 Hz, rates at which the left hemisphere reproduced all benchmarks; rate dependence is shown in Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 4a. A neuron was active if it fired at least once in any trial of a condition, so counts of active neurons grow slightly with the number of trials. A benchmark was counted as reproduced when MN9 fired above 20 Hz for sugar; suppression, 1 − (MN9 with sugar and bitter or Ir94e)/(MN9 with sugar alone), exceeded 50% and sugar alone was reproduced; ipsilateral aBN1 fired above 20 Hz; and contralateral aDN1 and aDN2 fired above 10 Hz. A condition was classed as runaway when more than 2,000 neurons were active. Runaway onset was read from cumulative recruitment curves as the time of the steep rise beyond the plateau reached by left-hemisphere stimulation. The route of the runaway was traced by ordering neurons by the time of their first spike in single trials.

Asymmetry analyses and corrections

Output and input synapse totals were computed from the connection table. For targeted deletion, each output synapse of the left sugar GRNs was removed independently with probability p (binomial thinning of connection counts; three random draws per p). For leave-one-out stimulation, each of the 24 right sugar GRNs was omitted in turn (four trials each). The antennal-lobe sign correction made all neurons of cell class ALLN (antennal-lobe local neurons) with predicted transmitter serotonin inhibitory (40 neurons). For input compensation, the weights of all output connections of each right stimulus population were multiplied by the ratio of left to right mean output synapses per neuron when this ratio exceeded 1; for readout compensation, the weights of all input connections of a right readout neuron were multiplied by the ratio of left to right input totals when it exceeded 1 (aBN1, ×3.99; MN9, ×1.06). The cell-type consensus correction assigned to every typed neuron the sign of the confidence-weighted majority of its type's transmitter predictions, pooled over both hemispheres.

Error models

Errors were applied to the intact v783 connection table and the left-hemisphere benchmarks were re-run (sugar, sugar with bitter, sugar with Ir94e at 100 Hz; JO at 150 Hz; eight trials each), with three random draws per error level. Missing synapses: each synapse was removed independently with probability p. Truncated dendrites or axons: each neuron lost a fraction f of its input (or output) synapses, with f drawn from a beta distribution with mean p and concentration 4, so that losses were correlated within neurons. Spurious synapses: q times the total number of synapses was added as single synapses between partners drawn in proportion to their existing output and input synapse totals; added synapses took the sign of their presynaptic neuron. Random sign errors: a fraction r of neurons with outputs switched between excitatory and inhibitory. Calibrated transmitter errors: each neuron kept its predicted transmitter with probability 1 − s(1 − c), where c is the prediction confidence2,4 and s a scale factor, and otherwise received a different transmitter drawn in proportion to transmitter frequencies; scales of 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 changed the sign of 6.1%, 12.2%, 24.3% and 47.0% of neurons (fractions of all neurons, averaged over draws), and at 0.25 the transmitter of 8.2%. Weight noise: each connection weight was multiplied by log-normal noise with mean 1 and standard deviation σ of the underlying normal distribution. We also tested discarding connections with fewer than k synapses and treating glutamatergic neurons as excitatory. Responses are expressed relative to the mean of three intact runs. Suppression was evaluated only when sugar alone drove MN9 above 20 Hz. The tolerance of a benchmark is the error level, interpolated linearly, at which its mean fell below half of its intact value. Brain-wide similarity is the Pearson correlation between log(1 + rate) of the perturbed and intact responses over neurons active in either, averaged over the four conditions.

Sensory mirror screen

We selected the 131 sensory cell types with at least three annotated neurons in each hemisphere (53 olfactory, 50 mechanosensory, 13 gustatory, 4 hygrosensory, 3 thermosensory, 3 visual and 5 unclassified types). Each type was stimulated at 100 Hz from each hemisphere separately (four trials) in the published connectome, after the antennal-lobe sign correction and after the additional cell-type consensus correction. Runaway was assessed on non-stimulated neurons. For each stimulation, responses were averaged by cell type and by side relative to the stimulus (ipsilateral or contralateral), giving a mirror-aligned response vector. Mirror concordance is the Pearson correlation between log(1 + rate) of the left- and right-stimulation vectors over entries active in either; types with fewer than three active entries were excluded. To estimate the agreement expected from trial-to-trial variability alone, each stimulation was repeated with new Poisson input, and within-side reproducibility was computed in the same way and averaged over the two sides. Mirror discordance is within-side reproducibility minus mirror concordance. The ipsilateral share is the summed firing of non-stimulated neurons in the stimulated hemisphere divided by that in both hemispheres.

Statistics and reproducibility

Each condition was simulated in 4–30 independent trials with different Poisson input; error models were drawn with three random seeds per level. Firing rates are trial means. Correlations are Pearson coefficients unless stated; Wilcoxon signed-rank tests are two-sided. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. All analyses are deterministic given the random seeds recorded in the code.
